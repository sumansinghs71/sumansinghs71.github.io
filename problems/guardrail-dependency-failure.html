Safety & Identity

⛨ The safety check failed because one of its own dependencies was down.

A guardrail is part of the production dependency graph; its outage policy must be explicit before the dependency fails.

What it looks like

  • Agent requests fail because the policy/guardrail service cannot reach an upstream dependency.
  • Teams debate during the incident whether to bypass the safety layer.
  • Low-risk and high-risk actions are blocked identically.

The failure boundary

When the safety decision is unavailable, the runtime still has to choose: fail closed, fail open, or enter a limited degraded mode. That choice is security policy—not an exception handler.

Why this happens

Guardrails may rely on identity, policy stores, classification services or third-party APIs. If the entire system assumes those dependencies are always available, an upstream outage can either stop the product or tempt operators to bypass controls under pressure.

A production-shaped example

A policy check depends on an external identity/data service. That service has a regional outage. Read-only summarization and high-impact write actions now both receive the same 'guardrail unavailable' error even though their risk is very different.

What fooled the team

  • Treating guardrails as stateless prompt text instead of a service dependency.
  • Using one global fail-open/fail-closed decision for every action class.
  • Allowing emergency bypasses that are not scoped, logged and time-bounded.
  • Testing malicious prompts but never testing guardrail infrastructure failure.

Prevent

  • Define availability policy by action risk: read, propose, reversible write, irreversible write.
  • Keep high-impact authorization enforcement in the runtime even when auxiliary classifiers are unavailable.
  • Cache only policies that are safe to use with explicit version/expiry semantics.
  • Reduce correlated dependencies between the agent and the service that authorizes it.

Detect

  • Separate 'request denied' from 'policy decision unavailable'.
  • Trace which upstream dependency prevented the guardrail decision.
  • Alert on bypass/degraded-mode activation.

Recover

  • Enter a pre-defined degraded mode rather than improvising during the outage.
  • Keep risky writes blocked unless an alternate authoritative control exists.
  • Revalidate deferred actions after policy service recovery.

Prove it with failure injection

  • Take the guardrail dependency offline during read-only tasks.
  • Repeat for reversible and irreversible actions.
  • Expire cached policy while the dependency is unavailable.
  • Verify emergency override scope, audit and automatic expiry.

What I would monitor

policy-unavailable rate
degraded-mode actions
blocked high-risk actions
override usage
cached-policy age

Production invariant

Loss of a safety dependency must never silently grant more authority than the healthy system would allow.
Takeaway: The guardrail has a failure mode too. Design that mode before someone asks to bypass it at 2 a.m.
