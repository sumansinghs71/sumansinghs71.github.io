Human & Operations

👤 Human review became the slowest service in the architecture

Approval gates reduce risk, but putting a person in every loop can turn safety into a queueing problem.

What it looks like

  • Approval queues grow faster than reviewers can clear them.
  • Agents finish quickly but tasks wait hours for a click.
  • Reviewers approve by habit because too many low-risk cases look the same.

Why this happens

Human-in-the-loop is often added as a universal safety answer. In production, human attention is limited and expensive. If every action requires the same level of review, high-risk cases wait behind routine ones and reviewers become numb to alerts. Long approval delays also make the underlying data and authorization stale.

A simple real-world example

A finance agent prepares 1,200 low-value invoice adjustments each day. Policy requires a manager to approve every one. The agent finishes in minutes; managers take hours. By the time an approval arrives, some invoices have already changed state. The control exists, but it is now both a bottleneck and a source of stale decisions.

What teams usually get wrong

  • Using the same approval rule for $5 and $500,000 actions.
  • Measuring agent latency without approval wait time.
  • Assuming more approvals always means more safety.
  • Giving reviewers no explanation, evidence or changed-field summary.

What I would put in production

  • Use risk-based approval thresholds and policy automation for low-risk cases.
  • Prioritize review queues by impact and uncertainty.
  • Show reviewers the evidence, exact proposed action and what changed.
  • Expire approvals when critical state becomes stale.
  • Measure reviewer disagreement and automate only where evidence supports it.

What I would monitor

approval queue age
human review time
override/rejection rate
stale approvals
reviewer load by risk tier
